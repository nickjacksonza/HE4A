## Key Takeaways

- The November 3 midterms changed the balance of power in Washington. Democrats won the House, and several outlets report they won the Senate too. The new Congress convenes January 3.
- Expect more oversight and more questions about how money is spent. Don't expect a return to the funding levels of 2024.
- Proposed cuts to HIV programs are still in the FY2027 budget process, so the next few months matter.
- Organizations that fix their governance, finances and leadership now will be ready when the money and the questions arrive.

The election is over, the votes are still being counted in a few places, and your funders are already asking what you'll do next. Strike while the iron is hot. The next few months are when your organization can change shape on its own terms, before the next funding cycle changes it for you.

Here's what we know, what we don't, and where to start.

## What Changed on November 3

Democrats won control of the House of Representatives. Several news outlets also report that they won the Senate, although a few races were still uncalled when we checked, and final margins aren't settled. The new Congress convenes on January 3.

Nonprofit leaders quoted in the Chronicle of Philanthropy say they hope for a more normal relationship with the federal government after a period of paused grants and executive orders barring federal support for diversity, equity and inclusion work and for programs serving immigrants and LGBTQ people. Akilah Watkins of Independent Sector said she expects a Democratic Congress to push to restore money for research, human services and safety-net programs.

Be careful with the word "restore." The president keeps a veto through January 2029, and the executive orders are still in place. Experts quoted by the Council on Foreign Relations expect the changes to come slowly, and Congress to focus first on oversight.

## What the Numbers Look Like

**Global HIV funding is smaller and moving through governments.** The State Department's America First Global Health Strategy works mainly through agreements with individual countries, not through international organizations or NGOs. About $16 billion is committed across 36 signed agreements, and KFF calculates that this represents a 34% drop in U.S. global health aid. Congress kept PEPFAR close to earlier levels, at $4.8 billion for fiscal year 2026, but the Global Fund's U.S. contribution was $1.25 billion, $400 million below the year before. A KFF analysis of 14 countries found that the disruption to PEPFAR funding was linked to drops in access to services from early 2025 into 2026, with limited recovery so far.

**Domestic HIV funding is still being fought over.** The White House's fiscal year 2027 request eliminates core CDC HIV prevention programs, keeping only the $220 million in Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) funds and proposing to move them to a new Administration for Healthy America. It also eliminates Part F of the Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program. The House's fiscal year 2027 Labor-HHS bill would cut the Ryan White program by $224.9 million from the 2026 level, according to the HIV Medicine Association. No final fiscal year 2027 bill had passed when we checked.

**Philanthropy is thin for LGBTQ+ work.** One summary of foundation data says LGBTQ causes received about $0.16 of every $100 that U.S. foundations awarded in 2024. Local funders are still giving. The Boston Foundation's Equality Fund, for example, announced $705,000 for 42 LGBTQ+-serving organizations in June 2026, with health care access as a priority. The Senate also passed funding in January 2026 that mandated the return of the LGBTQ youth service on the 988 crisis line.

## What to Expect From the New Congress

- **Oversight first.** Experts expect lawmakers to demand the signed agreements, implementation plans and detail on how money is spent. Jennifer Kates of KFF said she expects Congress to ask for them.
- **Partial restoration, not a reset.** Yanzhong Huang of the Council on Foreign Relations sees room for partial restoration of USAID funding and functions, and a full revival as unlikely soon.
- **Lower expectations on grant size.** Prashant Yadav, also at the Council on Foreign Relations, argues that grant levels won't return to earlier levels.
- **Friendlier committees.** The Washington Blade reported that several LGBTQ+ lawmakers could take committee leadership roles. Rep. Mark Pocan sits on the House Appropriations Committee and could influence funding for LGBTQ-related programs and challenges to HIV cuts. Sen. Tammy Baldwin has backed spending bills without anti-LGBTQ riders. Those outcomes weren't settled when the article ran.

## Why Reorganize Now

**Money is about to move, and it will favor the ready.** Appropriations decisions and new grant cycles will arrive in the coming months. The organizations that can show clean books, a strong board and a clear plan will be the first called.

**Oversight raises the bar.** If Congress asks the agencies for data and results, agencies will ask grantees for the same. Organizations that can't report outcomes clearly will struggle.

**The sector is changing shape.** With more global funding moving government to government, and domestic funding uncertain, organizations that depend on one source carry the most risk. Leaders have to decide whether to quietly absorb the pressure or take a public stand, as I wrote in January. Either decision is easier from a stable structure.

**Leaders are moving.** The HIV field has seen many executive directors leave over the past year. New leaders and their boards need a plan for the first hundred days.

## Where to Start

1. **Review your governance.** Does your board know who decides what? A [board governance review](/non-profit-leadership.html#board-governance-review) shows you where authority sits and gives you a plan for the next two board cycles.
2. **Write the succession plans.** An emergency plan for your executive director's absence, and a plan for the chair. Our post on [board succession and term limits](/insights/board-succession-term-limits-health-nonprofits.html) has the steps.
3. **Map your revenue.** List every source, how much of your budget it covers, and when it ends. Name one new source to pursue this quarter.
4. **Get your data ready.** Decide the three outcomes you'll report and how you'll prove them.
5. **Support your leaders.** [Executive coaching for HIV nonprofit leaders](/non-profit-leadership.html#executive-coaching) gives you a confidential place to work through decisions with no easy answer.
6. **Train your teams.** Make sure your staff can deliver affirming care and that your policies match your promises. See our [LGBTQ+ equity training for healthcare](/trainings.html).
7. **Build relationships across the aisle.** The Council on Foundations urged funders to build relationships with members of both parties. The same applies to you.

## A Word of Caution

Much of the commentary above comes from the first days after the election. Final numbers may change, committee assignments aren't set, and the executive branch still controls much of how money is spent. Plan for several outcomes. Don't wait for certainty, because it won't arrive before the opportunity does.

We'll update this post as the picture clears. If you'd like to talk through what reorganizing looks like for your organization, [start a conversation with us](/contact.html).
